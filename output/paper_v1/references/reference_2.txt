Abstract. We study vertex-restricted chain pair simplification, where two
polygonal curves are simplified simultaneously while their mutual corre-
spondence is preserved. Previous exact algorithms use the discrete Fréchet
distance for both fidelity to the input curves and synchronization be-
tween the two simplifications. We instead require continuous Fréchet fi-
delity from each input curve to its simplification, while retaining a dis-
crete Fréchet constraint between the two simplified curves. This hybrid
model captures the geometry of every simplified edge without losing the
vertex-to-vertex correspondence that motivates chain pair simplification.
Keywords: Chain Simplification · Fréchet Distance · c-Packed Curves.

Introduction
Polygonal curves are fundamental geometric structures used to model continu-
ous paths and complex trajectories across diverse applied domains. Their real-
world applications range from representing 3D topographic features in Geo-
graphic Information Systems (GIS) and GPS traces of moving vehicles, to mod-
eling the structural backbones of proteins in computational biology. Analyzing,
compressing, and finding correspondences between such geometric datasets
requires robust mathematical similarity measures.
A traditional metric is the Hausdorff distance. It measures the maximum dis-
tance from a point in one set to the nearest point in the other set, but ignores
the order in which a curve is traversed. The Fréchet distance is a natural alter-
native for ordered curves. It is often described by an analogy of a man and a
dog connected by a leash, each walking along a curve from its starting point
to its end point. Both the man and the dog can control their speed but they
are not allowed to backtrack. The Fréchet distance between the two curves is
the minimum leash length that is sufficient for traversing both curves in this
manner.
A simpler variant is the Discrete Fréchet distance. Instead of walking contin-
uously along the segments, the man and the dog behave like frogs, hopping
monotonically from one vertex to the next. While easier to compute, the discrete

considered a good approximation of the continuous distance. Both variants can
be computed in near-quadratic time [1, 2, 7, 10], and conditional lower bounds
rule out strongly subquadratic algorithms under standard assumptions [4, 6].
Raw geometric data can be dense, noisy, or redundantly sampled. Curve sim-
plification reduces its complexity by replacing a curve with a polygonal curve
having fewer vertices while preserving the relevant geometric structure. In this
paper, we use the vertex-restricted model, in which every vertex of a simplifi-
cation is an input vertex and their order is preserved.
While simplifying a single trajectory is a well-studied problem, processing pairs
of corresponding trajectories introduces severe synchronization challenges. The
need to simplify two curves jointly arises when their relation, rather than either
curve in isolation, is the object of interest. For example, protein backbones may
be compared after alignment, and two routes may be displayed together at a re-
duced level of detail, for a clearer visualization. In either setting, independently
simplifying the two curves can preserve each input while destroying the cor-
respondence between the outputs. Chain Pair Simplification (CPS), introduced
by Bereg et al. [3], addresses this by imposing both fidelity and coupling con-
straints on a pair of simplifications.

The specific choice of dsimp and dsync, whether it is Hausdorff, discrete Fréchet,
or continuous Fréchet determines the particular variant of the problem. The
choice of fidelity measure affects which vertices can be removed. The discrete
Fréchet distance requires a monotone matching between vertex sequences. The
continuous Fréchet distance allows matching to points in edge interiors. Both
respect traversal order, unlike the Hausdorff distance. Observe that for polyg-
onal curves it holds that dF(P, Q) ≤ddF(P, Q): every discrete coupling ex-
tends by linear interpolation to a continuous matching. Thus continuous fi-
delity can admit smaller simplifications at the same threshold. It does not im-
pose a stronger error bound than discrete fidelity.
In this paper, we study a hybrid CPS model, in which dsimp = dF is the con-
tinuous Fréchet distance and dsync = ddF is the discrete Fréchet distance. One
can picture the three constraints through a four-walker analogy: a walker on
each input curve is coupled continuously to a walker on its simplification, and
the two simplification walkers are coupled at their vertices. This combination
preserves the geometry of every shortcut edge and retains the vertex-to-vertex
synchronization that motivates CPS.
Our choice of distance measures reflects the different roles of simplification and
comparison. Simplification aims to summarize a curve and make its geometry
easier to visualize and interpret. Continuous Fréchet fidelity therefore provides
a natural criterion for assessing the geometric quality of the displayed simplifi-
cations. By allowing input vertices to match points inside shortcut edges, it can
produce more compact representations at the same error threshold, potentially
improving visual clarity and yielding shorter curves.
The comparison between the two simplifications serves a different purpose:
here, the correspondence between selected vertices matters. We therefore con-
tinue to select output vertices from the original curves A and B, and mea-
sure the distance between the simplification curves A′ and B′ using the dis-
crete Fréchet distance. This choice is meaningful, for example, when comparing
protein backbones, whose vertices represent α-carbon atoms: the discrete cou-
pling matches selected atoms to selected atoms, rather than to arbitrary points

Preliminaries and Problem definition
A polygonal curve A = ⟨a1, a2, . . . , am⟩is represented by a sequence of its ver-
tices. We can also treat A as a function: A : [1, m] →Rd, where the vertices
are A[i] = ai, for 1 ≤i ≤m and the edge between ai and ai+1 is A[i, i + 1]. A
subcurve from index i to j is denoted by A[i, j]. We use the analogous notation
for a second curve B = ⟨b1, . . . , bn⟩.
The continuous Fréchet distance (dF) between two curves A and B is defined
as the infimum, over all monotone reparameterizations of the two curves, of
the maximum distance between matched points. Let A and B be two curves
A : [1, m] →Rd and B : [1, n] →Rd. The continuous Fréchet distance between
them is defined as:
dF(A, B) = inf
σ,θ max
t∈[0,1] ∥A(σ(t)) −B(θ(t))∥,
where σ and θ are continuous non-decreasing surjections from [0, 1] to [1, m]
and [1, n], respectively, describing the motion along A and B. Informally, we
can think of the parameter t as "time". Then, A(σ(t)) is the position of the dog
and B(θ(t)) is the position of the man at time t.
From a geometric perspective, stating that dF(A, B) ≤δ is equivalent to saying
that there exist a traversal of the curves such that at any point in time t, the
point on A lies within a disk of radius δ centered at the corresponding point on
B.

Cost Function. The cost of a transition is determined by counting how many

the edge weight is wA(e) = 1; otherwise, it is zero. In the same way, if q < q′, a

∑e∈π wB(e), respectively.
Our objective is to find a path π in G from the start to the end that minimizes
the expression max(wA(π), wB(π)).

configuration of the form (1, p, 1, q) is considered a potential initial configura-

tion, so that each such configuration becomes a root in the topologically sorted
sequence. Then, in Step 2 of Algorithm ?? we initialize the arrays of every s ∈S,
in total time O(m2n), and in Step 3 we process only the vertices that are not in
S. The value Xv[r] for such a vertex v is now the minimum number z such that
v can be reached with r hops of A′ and z hops of B′, over all potential initial
configurations s ∈S. In the final step we compute minr max(r, Xt[r]) for each
potential final configuration t ∈T, in O(m) time each, and return the smallest
value obtained; since |T| = O(mn), this step takes O(m2n) time. Both modifica-
tions are dominated by the running time of the dynamic program itself, so the
running time of the entire algorithm is unchanged.

Realistic Scenarios
While the polynomial bounds guarantee an optimal solution, they are heavily
influenced by pathological cases, such as trajectories with extreme zig-zags or
dense spiral patterns, which force a massive number of auxiliary points (Vaux).
However, real-world data rarely exhibits such worst-case geometric behavior.
We can bridge the gap between theoretical and practical efficiency by introduc-
ing the framework of c-packed curves. The concept of c-packedness provides a
realistic mathematical model to capture the behavior of practical trajectories.
Definition: A curve is called c-packed if the total length of its intersection with
any disk of radius r (in the plane) is strictly bounded by c · r, where c is a posi-
tive real constant [9].
Essentially, the parameter c measures the congestion of the curve. A low value
of c implies a well-behaved curve that travels reasonably straight and escapes
local neighborhoods efficiently, whereas a high value of c indicates dense, highly
convoluted movement within small regions.
